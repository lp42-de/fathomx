Seventy-one percent of this planet is ocean. It belongs to no nation. It receives more sunlight than any desert. Its winds blow day and night without stopping. Its waves carry energy that has traveled three thousand miles undisturbed.

The North Sea, the Persian Gulf, the Bass Strait — every offshore energy project in history has been built inside someone's waters, under someone's rules, revocable by someone's government. The open ocean has none of that. It has never been used to generate energy at commercial scale. Not because it can't be. Because nobody has gone there yet.

The platform

Collect. Convert. Deliver.

A floating platform anchored in international waters. Three energy sources. One cable back to the mainland.

01

Solar

The open ocean sits beneath some of the highest solar irradiance on the planet. No terrain blocking it. No one to ask permission. The sun shows up whether you have permits or not.

02

Wind

The trade winds that guided sailors across oceans for five hundred years blow 24 hours a day. Floating turbines — proven at commercial scale since 2017 — turn them into electricity far beyond any coast.

03

Wave

Swell that crossed three thousand miles of open ocean arrives at the hull with energy still in it. Wave converters built into the mooring capture what doesn't stop when the sun sets.

The same submarine cable technology that already links the UK to Norway carries electricity from the platform to a national grid. We use it to cross a different kind of border.

B

Compute at sea

The cable carries compute inbound. Servers running on clean, cheap, unrestricted offshore power — available to anyone, answerable to no one. A data center with no landlord and no government looking in.

C

Food from open water

The exclusion zone around the platform becomes a marine reserve. Offshore aquaculture — running on platform power, staffed by the crew — adds a third revenue stream alongside energy and compute.

Why now

The window is open.
Not for long.

The technology to do this has existed for about five years. The legal framework governing international waters is stable enough to operate under. The demand for clean, independent energy from nations that import it is growing every year.

There is no incumbent. There is no one to displace. The ocean is not a crowded market — it is an empty one. The first company to land a cable from international waters to a mainland grid owns that position for the life of the asset. That asset lasts forty years.

Every piece already exists.

Floating turbines operate commercially off Scotland and Norway. Submarine cables carry a gigawatt across 720 kilometers in the North Sea. Offshore structures have survived Category 5 hurricanes for decades in the Gulf of Mexico. The components are proven. Nobody has assembled them beyond the 200-mile line.

Half the world is paying too much for power.

Island nations run generators at $0.40 per kilowatt-hour — four times what most Americans pay. Fast-growing economies import energy from neighbors they don't fully trust. A supply agreement with no geopolitical strings, 25 years at a fixed price, changes the calculation permanently.

The ocean is the moat.

A platform in international waters can't be zoned out, eminent-domained, or have its license pulled after an election. Nobody can undercut you on location. The competitive position is geographic — and the ocean isn't going anywhere.
