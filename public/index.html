The thesis

The last untapped energy frontier is the open ocean.

Every major energy resource on land is spoken for — jurisdictionally, politically, or commercially. The high seas are not. International waters sit under no nation's authority, suffer no permitting delays, and receive more solar irradiance and consistent wind than any land-locked alternative. The only thing missing is the infrastructure to harvest it.

FathomX is building that infrastructure — and the first company to successfully transmit offshore international-waters power to a mainland grid will own an energy franchise with no analog in history.

No landlord. No lease. No expiry.

Under UNCLOS, the high seas belong to no nation. A floating platform registered under a flag state operates in perpetuity without zoning risk, compulsory acquisition, or sovereign resource nationalism.

The best wind on Earth is unoccupied.

The Southeast Trade Winds and the Arabian Sea monsoon belt deliver 7–9 m/s annual average winds with capacity factors of 38–46% — higher than most European offshore wind projects already in operation.

HVDC cables make the distance irrelevant.

High-voltage direct current submarine cables now transmit power with less than 3.5% loss over 700 km. The North Sea Link has been doing exactly this commercially since 2021.

AI compute multiplies the revenue model.

A 10 MW GPU cluster co-located on the platform uses stranded power at near-zero marginal cost — generating a second revenue stream in offshore AI inference with no additional cable or land rights required.

The platform

Three energy types. One modular platform.

FathomX platforms are floating semi-submersible structures anchored by suction caissons in 2,000–4,000m water. Each module can be independently added, replaced, or upgraded — no dry-dock required.

☀️

Solar

Marine-grade bifacial panels mounted on the platform deck, tilted to follow optimal irradiance angles. The Arabian Sea and Eastern Pacific zones receive 2,000–2,350 kWh/m²/yr — among the highest ocean GHI values globally.

Wind

15 MW floating offshore turbines mounted on the platform's semi-submersible hull. Floating wind is proven at utility scale — Hywind Tampen (88 MW) has operated commercially since 2023.

38–46% capacity factor at target sites

Wave

Point-absorber wave energy converters integrated into the mooring system capture residual energy from ocean swell. Wave energy is modular and additive — Phase 2 technology once the platform is established.

Why now

Five forces that make this the right decade.

01

Floating wind is proven

Hywind Tampen (88 MW) has operated commercially since 2023. The technology is ready. The supply chain is scaling. The window to be first in international waters is open — and closing.

02

HVDC has reached commercial scale

The North Sea Link (720 km, 1,400 MW) has been operating since 2021. The Xlinks Morocco-UK project (3,800 km) is in financing. Submarine HVDC is no longer experimental — it is infrastructure finance.

03

AI compute demand is insatiable

Global data center power demand is projected to double by 2030. Power is the binding constraint on AI scaling. A platform that generates cheap power in international waters can run GPUs at costs no land-based operator can match.

04

Energy-importing nations are desperate for supply

India adds 150–200 TWh of demand annually. Island nations pay $0.25–0.50/kWh for diesel generation. An offshore clean energy supplier offering $85–100/MWh PPAs solves a real and urgent problem.

05

The BBNJ Treaty creates a governance framework

The High Seas Treaty (adopted March 2023) will formalize environmental standards for activities in international waters — creating regulatory clarity for the first time. Early movers who conduct voluntary EIAs now will be positioned when the framework arrives.

06

No incumbent exists

Every other energy infrastructure category on Earth has entrenched operators. The open ocean does not. The first company to land a cable from international waters to a mainland grid owns a position that cannot be replicated.
