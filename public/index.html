The opportunity

Every offshore energy project in history has been built

The open ocean — 110 million square miles beyond the 200-mile line — has never been used to generate energy at scale. Not because it can't be done. Because nobody has gone there yet.

There's no coastal state permit, no energy policy election cycle, no landlord. You register the structure as a vessel or fixed installation, comply with MARPOL and SOLAS, and operate. The legal framework is mature. The ocean engineering is mature. Nobody has assembled both in international waters for energy production.
Submarine HVDC cable — the same technology that carries 700 megawatts under the North Sea between Norway and the Netherlands (720 km). The NorNed cable has been operating since 2008. A platform 380 miles from an island grid with a $0.40/kWh diesel price has enormous economic incentive to pay for the cable. First deployments target island nations and archipelago states with the highest existing electricity costs.
Offshore oil and gas platforms survive Category 5 hurricanes. The Sleipner platform in the North Sea has operated continuously since 1996. Salt corrosion is a solved engineering problem — the materials, coatings, and maintenance protocols exist. The cost premium for offshore durability is real and is already priced into the economics. At $0.40/kWh competing cost, there is substantial margin to absorb it.
Three reasons. First, offshore energy has historically focused on fossil fuels, which have their own extraction rationale. Second, renewable energy technology only reached offshore cost-competitiveness in the last decade. Third, the 200-mile limit means you're operating in waters with no obvious government sponsor, which means no government subsidy and no government mandate — the project has to be commercially self-sustaining from day one. That's a harder pitch to institutional investors than a grid-connected wind farm with a 20-year feed-in tariff. We think that's the opportunity, not the obstacle.
