Yes — and it's the point. Beyond 200 nautical miles from any coast, UNCLOS governs. Article 87(1)(d) explicitly grants freedom to construct artificial islands and installations. There's no coastal state permit, no energy policy election cycle, no landlord. You register the structure as a vessel or fixed installation, comply with MARPOL and SOLAS, and operate. The legal framework is mature. Nobody has used it for energy production.
Submarine HVDC cable — the same technology that carries 700 megawatts under the North Sea between Norway and the Netherlands (720 km). The NorNed cable has been operating since 2008. A platform 380 miles from an island grid with a $0.40/kWh diesel price has enormous economic incentive to pay for the cable. First deployments target island nations and archipelago states with the highest existing electricity costs.
Offshore oil and gas platforms survive Category 5 hurricanes. The Sleipner platform in the North Sea has operated continuously since 1996. Salt corrosion is a solved engineering problem — the materials, coatings, and maintenance protocols exist. The cost premium for offshore durability is real and is already priced into the economics. At $0.40/kWh competing cost, there is substantial margin to absorb it.
Three reasons. First, offshore energy has historically focused on fossil fuels, which have their own extraction rationale inside EEZs. Second, renewable energy technology only reached offshore cost-competitiveness in the last decade. Third, operating beyond the 200-mile limit means no government sponsor, no subsidy, and no mandate — the project must be commercially self-sustaining from day one. That's a harder pitch than a grid-connected wind farm with a 20-year feed-in tariff. We think that's the opportunity, not the obstacle.
The capital stack looks like infrastructure finance, not venture capital. A signed PPA with an island government is the anchor — it turns the project into a long-duration contracted cash flow, which is what infrastructure funds, sovereign wealth, and development finance institutions (ADB, IFC, DFIs) invest in. The pre-commercial phase (concept through feasibility study) is founder-funded and grant-eligible through programs targeting energy access and climate transition. We're not raising venture money to build this; we're building the offtake agreement that makes the project financeable.
Pacific island nations are the primary first market. Micronesia, the Marshall Islands, Palau, and Guam all pay $0.35–0.52/kWh for diesel-generated electricity and have no realistic alternative — they're too small for conventional offshore wind projects, too remote for grid interconnection. The Maldives and select Caribbean island states are secondary targets. All have diesel dependency, government-owned utilities with authority to sign a PPA, and are within 400 nautical miles of viable platform sites.
