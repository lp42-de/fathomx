Seventy-one percent of this planet is ocean. It receives more sunlight than any desert. Its winds blow every hour of every day. Its waves carry energy that has crossed entire ocean basins undisturbed. And it belongs to no one.

The North Sea. The Persian Gulf. The Bass Strait. Every offshore energy project in history has been built inside someone's waters, under someone's rules, at someone else's pleasure. The open ocean — 45% of the Earth's surface — has never been used to generate energy at scale. Not because it can't be. Because nobody has built there yet.

Collect. Convert. Deliver.

A floating platform anchored in international waters harvests from every direction — sun, wind, wave — and sends the output back through a submarine cable to any grid willing to receive it.

The window
is open.
Not forever.

Every category of energy infrastructure that exists today has entrenched operators, established regulations, and incumbents with decades of sunk capital defending their position.

International waters has none of that. The legal framework is still forming. The supply chain is just reaching readiness. The first company to transmit power from international waters to a mainland grid will own a position that cannot be replicated — because the ocean doesn't run out of space, but the window for being first does close.

Every piece already exists.

Floating wind farms operate commercially. Submarine cables carry a gigawatt across 700 kilometers. Offshore structures have survived Category 5 weather for decades in the Gulf of Mexico. The components have been built and proven in isolation. Nobody has assembled them beyond the 200-mile line.

Half the world pays too much for power.

Island nations run on diesel at prices that would be a political crisis on the mainland. Fast-growing economies import energy from neighbors they don't fully trust. A clean, independent offshore energy source — with no geopolitical strings and a 25-year supply agreement — changes the calculus for both.

The moat is the ocean itself.

A platform in international waters can't be zoned out, subject to eminent domain, or have its license revoked by an election. The ocean is not a temporary competitive advantage. It is a permanent one. Whoever builds first keeps that position for the life of the asset — which is forty years.
